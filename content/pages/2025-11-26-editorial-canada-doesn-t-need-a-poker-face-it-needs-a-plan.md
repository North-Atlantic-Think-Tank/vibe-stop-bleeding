# Editorial: Canada Doesn't Need a Poker Face—It Needs a Plan

*The Carney government's dual-track approach to the United States reveals a deeper failure of Canadian political leadership*

---

There's a word for saying one thing while doing another: hypocrisy. In politics, we often dress it up with euphemisms—"strategic flexibility," "diplomatic nuance," "complex balancing act." But our investigation into Prime Minister Mark Carney's first six months in office reveals something Canadians need to confront directly: **we elected a leader who promised defiance and delivered deference, who campaigns like a hockey enforcer but governs like an accountant.**

This isn't necessarily a scandal. But it is a betrayal of the democratic contract.

## The Dangerous Game of Having It Both Ways

Let's be clear about what's happening. Mark Carney is attempting to simultaneously:

- Tell Canadians the "old relationship is over" while negotiating within its framework
- Declare "the world can move on without the US" while depending on America for three-quarters of our exports
- Promise "elbows up" resistance while apologizing to Donald Trump for quoting Ronald Reagan
- Position Canada as a champion of multilateralism while accommodating unilateral American demands

Politicians have always tried to be all things to all people. But Carney's contradiction runs deeper than typical political positioning—it represents a fundamental confusion about Canada's place in the world, or worse, a calculated decision to mislead voters about the choices we actually face.

The question isn't whether Carney is right to accommodate American economic power—given our export dependence, he may have no choice. **The question is why he won't be honest about it.**

## The Moral Case for Honest Politics

Democracy requires informed consent. When Canadians voted in April 2025, they were voting on a specific question: Do we stand up to Trump, or do we accommodate him? Carney promised the former. He's delivering the latter.

This isn't about policy outcomes—reasonable people can disagree about whether trade diversification or economic realism should guide Canadian strategy. This is about **the integrity of the political process itself.**

If Carney believed that Canada's economic vulnerability requires compromise with the United States, he should have campaigned on that reality. "We'll stand firm where we can, but our workers' jobs depend on pragmatic cooperation." That would have been honest. Uncomfortable, perhaps. Politically risky, certainly. But honest.

Instead, Carney offered Canadians a fantasy—that we could have both economic security and moral righteousness, that "elbows up" rhetoric would somehow insulate us from the hard choices that small, trade-dependent nations face when their largest partner turns hostile.

The result? Canadians are now discovering that the leader they elected doesn't actually exist.

## Why This Matters More Than You Think

Some will argue this is politics as usual, that all leaders moderate their positions after taking office, that campaign promises are aspirational rather than binding. This defense misses the point.

Carney's contradiction isn't just politically problematic—**it's strategically dangerous.**

**First, it undermines negotiating credibility.** If Donald Trump knows that Carney's tough talk is just for domestic consumption, why would he take Canadian red lines seriously? Every time Carney declares the relationship "over" and then backs down, he teaches the Americans that Canadian resolve is performative. We become easier to push around, not harder.

**Second, it prevents genuine public debate.** If Carney were honest about the trade-offs—"diversification will take a decade and require economic pain; accommodation preserves jobs but limits sovereignty"—Canadians could have a real conversation about which path to choose. Instead, we're having a fake debate about a fake strategy.

**Third, it sets Canada up for crisis.** Contradictions don't resolve themselves—they escalate until something breaks. Either Trump will call Carney's bluff with devastating tariffs, or Canadian voters will punish Carney for broken promises. The current equilibrium is unstable by design.

## What Should Carney Have Done?

Imagine an alternative approach. Picture Mark Carney standing before Canadians in April 2025 and saying:

*"Donald Trump's tariffs have exposed a hard truth: Canada is too dependent on the United States. Over the next decade, we will cut that dependence in half through aggressive trade diversification. This won't be easy or cheap. Your consumer goods will cost more. Some jobs in export sectors will disappear before new ones emerge. Companies will face uncertainty.*

*But the alternative is permanent vulnerability—a Canada that can be economically coerced whenever Washington decides to flex its muscles. That's not independence. That's vassalage with better PR.*

*So here's the deal: I will negotiate the best terms I can with the Americans to protect Canadian workers during this transition. I won't pick unnecessary fights or match Trump's insults. But I will invest heavily in Asian and European markets, and I will accept short-term economic pain for long-term sovereignty.*

*This is the choice: discomfort now for independence later, or comfort now and permanent dependence forever. I'm choosing independence. Will you join me?"*

Would Canadians have voted for that candidate? Maybe, maybe not. But at least they would have known what they were voting for.

## The Trade Diversification Mirage

Let's address the elephant in the room: **Carney's trade diversification strategy is mathematically implausible in the timeframe he's suggesting.**

Doubling non-US exports from $100 billion to $400 billion over ten years requires adding $30 billion annually. That's not just ambitious—it requires fundamentally restructuring the Canadian economy, building new supply chains, developing new products for different markets, and competing against established exporters who have decades-long relationships in those regions.

South Korea—one of the most successful trade diversification stories—took 30 years to reduce US export dependence from 40% to 15%. Canada is starting at 75%.

The $5 billion Trade Diversification Corridor Fund is a rounding error compared to the $575 billion Canada currently exports to the United States. The UAE trade agreement is welcome but tiny. ASEAN talks are preliminary.

None of this means diversification isn't worth pursuing. **But it does mean Carney's timeline is fantasy.** Real diversification will take 20-30 years of sustained effort, multiple governments, and fundamental economic transformation.

Which brings us back to the central dishonesty: If diversification is a generation-long project, Carney has no business pretending Canada can stand independent of the United States *right now*. The accommodation isn't a bridge to sovereignty—it's the reality for the foreseeable future.

## What Canadians Should Demand

We deserve better than strategic ambiguity that's really just political cowardice. Here's what Canadians should demand from the Carney government:

**1. Truth about the trade-offs.** Stop pretending we can have economic security and complete independence simultaneously. Tell us what accommodating the US will cost in sovereignty, and what pursuing sovereignty will cost in prosperity.

**2. Realistic timelines.** If trade diversification takes 30 years, say so. If current export dependence means we can't afford a trade war, admit it.

**3. Democratic accountability.** Let Parliament vote on the major policy reversals—removing counter-tariffs, increasing military spending, joining missile defense. These aren't technical adjustments; they're fundamental strategic choices.

**4. Consistent messaging.** Either we're pursuing strategic independence (accept the economic costs), or we're maintaining pragmatic cooperation (accept the sovereignty limits). Pick one.

**5. Contingency planning.** What happens when Trump retaliates to the G20 rhetoric? What happens when Canadian voters realize "elbows up" was a campaign slogan, not a governing philosophy? Have plans for both.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here's what many Canadians don't want to hear: **small countries don't have the luxury of moral posturing.**

When you're economically integrated with a superpower, when 75% of your exports go to a single market, when your population is one-tenth the size of your neighbor's, you don't get to play the role of defiant hero. You get to make smart compromises or stupid ones, but you're making compromises either way.

The tragedy isn't that Mark Carney is accommodating the United States. The tragedy is that he won an election by pretending he wouldn't have to.

Canada needs leaders who tell hard truths, not ones who sell comforting lies. We need politicians who respect voters enough to explain the actual choices we face, not ones who think we're children who need to be protected from reality.

## A Path Forward

Carney still has time to correct course. Here's how:

**Acknowledge the contradiction.** Give a major address explaining that campaign rhetoric didn't survive contact with governing reality. Canadians respect honesty about mistakes more than doubling down on obvious contradictions.

**Choose a coherent strategy.** Either commit to long-term diversification with transparent timelines and cost projections, or commit to pragmatic US cooperation with clear red lines for what Canada won't compromise.

**Rebuild negotiating credibility.** Stop the performative tough talk. If Canada makes a commitment to the Americans, keep it. If we draw a red line, defend it. Inconsistency makes us weaker, not stronger.

**Engage Canadians in real debate.** Commission a comprehensive study on what genuine independence from US economic dependence would require—costs, timeline, jobs impact, everything. Let Canadians decide if they're willing to pay that price.

**Prepare for consequences.** Trump will likely retaliate to the G20 statements. Have a plan that's more sophisticated than "talk tough, then back down."

## The Stakes

This isn't just about one prime minister's political survival. This is about whether Canadian democracy can function honestly when facing hard choices.

If voters learn that campaign promises are meaningless, that politicians will say whatever wins elections regardless of governing reality, we accelerate the cynicism that's already corroding democratic institutions. Why vote if the person you elected bears no resemblance to the person who governs?

Conversely, if we demand and reward honest leadership—even when the truth is uncomfortable—we strengthen democracy's foundation. We prove that voters can handle complexity, that politicians don't need to lie to win, that informed consent still matters.

## Our Verdict

Mark Carney is a smart, capable technocrat facing an extraordinarily difficult situation. Donald Trump's unreliability, America's growing unilateralism, and Canada's economic vulnerability create genuine strategic dilemmas with no easy answers.

But leadership isn't about avoiding hard choices—it's about making them clearly and owning the consequences.

Carney promised Canadians "elbows up." He's delivering "head down." That's not strategic ambiguity—that's broken faith with voters.

Canada doesn't need a prime minister who's trying to be two different leaders simultaneously. We need one who tells us the truth about the choices we face, argues honestly for the path he thinks is right, and lets Canadians decide if they agree.

The contradiction at the heart of the Carney government can't last. The only question is whether it ends with the prime minister choosing clarity, or with Canadians choosing someone else.

**It's time to pick a lane, Mr. Prime Minister. Canada is waiting.**
