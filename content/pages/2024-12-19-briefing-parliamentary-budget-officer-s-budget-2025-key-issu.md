# Briefing: Parliamentary Budget Officer's Budget 2025 Key Issues Analysis

*The Parliamentary Budget Officer identifies critical fiscal challenges facing Canada's 2025 budget, including a $46.8 billion deficit projection, rising debt servicing costs reaching $53.7 billion, and significant spending pressures across defence, pharmacare, and indigenous programs.*

---

## Context

The Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO) has released its pre-budget analysis identifying key fiscal issues parliamentarians should consider for Budget 2025. This briefing comes as Canada faces mounting economic pressures, elevated debt levels, and new spending commitments that will significantly impact the federal fiscal framework.

---

## Key Facts & Findings

### 1. **Deficit Trajectory Worsening**
- **FY 2024-25 Deficit**: Projected at $46.8 billion (1.6% of GDP)
- **Five-Year Outlook**: Cumulative deficit expected to reach $152.4 billion through 2028-29
- **Debt-to-GDP Ratio**: Rising from 41.9% (2023-24) to 43.1% (2028-29)
- **Context**: This represents a significant deterioration from the government's fiscal anchor commitment to maintain declining debt ratios

### 2. **Debt Servicing Costs Skyrocketing**
- **Current Year**: $53.7 billion in public debt charges for 2024-25
- **Growth Rate**: Representing a 55% increase since 2019-20
- **Percentage of Revenue**: Debt servicing now consumes 11.8% of total federal revenues
- **Implication**: These costs now exceed spending on Employment Insurance and are approaching Old Age Security expenditures

### 3. **Defence Spending Gap Critical**
- **NATO Target**: 2% of GDP commitment by 2032
- **Current Level**: 1.37% of GDP (2024-25)
- **Required Increase**: Additional $18.2 billion annually by 2032-33
- **Cumulative Cost**: $81.9 billion in new spending over eight years to meet NATO obligations
- **Challenge**: No clear funding pathway identified in current fiscal framework

### 4. **Pharmacare Program Cost Uncertainty**
- **Estimated Range**: $11.2 billion to $13.4 billion annually once fully implemented
- **Timing**: Full implementation expected by 2027
- **Budget Allocation**: Only $1.5 billion set aside over five years
- **Funding Gap**: Potential shortfall of $50+ billion over the program's first decade
- **Risk Factor**: Cost projections highly sensitive to coverage scope and utilization rates

### 5. **Indigenous Reconciliation Spending Pressures**
- **Child Welfare Settlement**: $23 billion long-term financial agreement (2023)
- **Ongoing Commitments**: Multiple outstanding claims and treaty obligations
- **Annual Growth**: Indigenous Services Canada budget increasing 8-10% annually
- **Sustainability Concern**: No clear framework for managing escalating costs while meeting reconciliation commitments

### 6. **Revenue Challenges Mounting**
- **Tax Revenue Growth**: Slowing significantly due to economic softening
- **Personal Income Tax**: Growth rate declining from 7.8% (2022-23) to projected 2.1% (2024-25)
- **Corporate Tax**: Volatile, dependent on commodity prices and corporate profitability
- **GST Revenue**: Growing below inflation due to consumption patterns shifting to services

### 7. **Economic Uncertainty Elevated**
- **GDP Growth**: Projected at 1.8% for 2025, below historical average
- **Unemployment Risk**: Rising from 5.4% to 6.5% through 2025
- **Inflation Persistence**: Core inflation remaining above Bank of Canada target
- **US Policy Risk**: Potential trade policy changes under new administration pose significant downside risks

---

## Key Conclusions & Implications

### 1. **Fiscal Room Severely Constrained**
The government faces approximately $100 billion in unfunded commitments (NATO, pharmacare, indigenous programs) over the next five years while operating with structural deficits. New spending initiatives in Budget 2025 will require either significant revenue increases or reallocation from existing programs.

### 2. **Debt Sustainability Becoming Central Issue**
With debt servicing costs consuming nearly 12% of revenues and projected to grow, Canada's fiscal flexibility is diminishing. The rising debt-to-GDP ratio violates the government's stated fiscal anchor, requiring either policy adjustment or formal abandonment of this commitment.

### 3. **Difficult Trade-offs Ahead**
Parliamentarians must confront explicit choices between:
- Meeting NATO commitments vs. domestic social spending
- Implementing pharmacare as promised vs. deficit reduction
- Funding indigenous reconciliation adequately vs. fiscal sustainability
- Maintaining current service levels vs. tax increases

### 4. **Revenue Enhancement Likely Necessary**
Given spending pressures and commitment to social programs, Budget 2025 may need to include revenue measures. Options include corporate tax adjustments, high-income earner surtaxes, or consumption tax changes.

### 5. **Transparency and Accountability Critical**
The PBO emphasizes the need for clear, honest accounting of long-term costs and funding sources for major initiatives. Parliamentarians should demand detailed fiscal frameworks for all new multi-year commitments to enable informed decision-making.

---

## Bottom Line

Budget 2025 arrives at a fiscal crossroads. With limited financial flexibility, rising debt costs, and major unfunded commitments, the government must make explicit choices about priorities and be transparent about trade-offs. The days of accommodating new spending through modest deficit increases appear over—structural fiscal adjustment is now required.
