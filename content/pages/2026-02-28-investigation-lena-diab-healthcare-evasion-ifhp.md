# Laughing While Canadians Wait: Minister Diab's Healthcare Evasion and the IFHP Cover-Up

**By the Investigative Team | stopbleeding.ca | February 28, 2026**

---

## The Moment That Revealed Everything

The question was direct. With six million Canadians unable to find a family doctor, emergency departments closing in communities from Northern Ontario to rural British Columbia, and a federal healthcare program extending benefits to people who have been told by Canada's own legal system they have no right to be here — someone at the Parliamentary Health Committee needed to ask the obvious question.

How many more patients can Canada's healthcare system actually handle?

Minister Lena Diab, Mark Carney's Immigration Minister, laughed.

"That is not a fair question for immigration!" she said.

In that single moment — a laugh and a deflection — Canadians got a clearer picture of this government's approach to the intersection of immigration and healthcare than any policy document could provide. The Minister responsible for making decisions about who enters and stays in Canada, and what federal benefits they receive, had just declared healthcare capacity irrelevant to her portfolio.

Six million Canadians without a family doctor would likely disagree.

---

## The Program the Minister Misrepresented

The Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP) is a federal initiative that provides healthcare coverage to asylum claimants, refugee claimants, and certain other non-citizen categories while their immigration status is being determined. The program's existence is not in dispute — in fact, it serves a legitimate humanitarian purpose for people awaiting determination of their refugee claims.

What is very much in dispute is what Minister Diab told the Parliamentary Health Committee about who is eligible.

The Minister stated that rejected asylum claimants — people who have gone through Canada's refugee determination process and been told by an Immigration and Refugee Board adjudicator that they do not qualify for protection — are not eligible for premium IFHP benefits.

This claim was wrong. And the evidence proving it wrong comes not from opposition researchers or political adversaries, but from two sources the government cannot dismiss: the Parliamentary Budget Officer and her own department's published program documentation.

Health Canada and IRCC's IFHP program guidelines, available publicly on Canada.ca, describe coverage tiers that include what the program terms "Comprehensive Benefits" and an "Expanded" tier that includes supplemental coverage such as dental care, vision care, prescription medications, and mental health services. These are benefits that millions of working Canadians pay for privately, go without, or access only through employer benefit plans — if they're lucky enough to have one.

Critically, the program documentation does not exclude rejected claimants from all coverage during the period they remain in Canada pending removal. The Parliamentary Budget Officer's analysis of IFHP costs has flagged this population as part of the program's fiscal footprint.

When a government minister makes a statement to a Parliamentary committee about program eligibility that is contradicted by the Parliamentary Budget Officer and the minister's own department's program documentation, there is a word for that. It is not "misremembering." It is not "imprecision." The word is: misrepresentation.

---

## The Coverage Gap That Offends Common Sense

Let us be precise about the grievance, because precision matters here.

Canada has a healthcare system that Canadians rightly prize. But that system — funded by Canadian taxpayers — does not include universal dental coverage for Canadians. It does not include universal vision care. It does not include universal prescription drug coverage. The federal dental benefit that the previous Trudeau government implemented is income-tested and limited. Provincial drug plans vary enormously.

A Canadian citizen earning $55,000 a year with no employer benefits may go without dental care for years. They may ration medications. They may not get new glasses when their prescription changes.

The IFHP — in its expanded tier — may provide those benefits to a rejected asylum claimant who has been ordered removed from Canada but remains here because the removal system cannot keep up with its own orders.

This is not a hypothetical disparity. This is the documented policy structure.

Conservative MP Michelle Rempel Garner has stated this plainly: "Not a single non-citizen without a legal reason to be in Canada should receive better healthcare than Canadian citizens." That principle is not radical. It is not xenophobic. It is the common-sense proposition that a country's publicly funded systems should prioritize the people who belong to and contribute to that country — before extending premium benefits to those who have been told, through due process, that they don't qualify for the protection they sought.

The counterargument — that removing these benefits would be cruel — deserves engagement, not dismissal. The answer is not to extend premium benefits to rejected claimants indefinitely. The answer is to remove rejected claimants from Canada promptly, as the law requires. Canada's failure to enforce its own removal orders is a policy failure that creates this dilemma — and it is a failure the government has shown little urgency to address.

---

## The Judges Who Found the Loophole

The healthcare question does not exist in isolation. It sits within a broader pattern of immigration system failures that this government has either caused or refused to fix.

Under Canada's Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, any non-citizen sentenced to six months or more in prison for a serious criminal offence becomes subject to mandatory deportation proceedings. Parliament was explicit: people who come to Canada and commit serious crimes can be removed. It is not optional. It is not discretionary. The law is clear.

Except it has become, effectively, optional.

Michelle Rempel Garner and legal observers have documented a pattern that should concern every Canadian: defence lawyers for non-citizen offenders are explicitly arguing for sentences calibrated just below the six-month threshold — and judges, aware that a sentence of six months means deportation, are granting those arguments. A sentence of five months and 29 days instead of seven months. The prison time is marginally different. The immigration consequence is everything.

This is judicial nullification of Parliamentary intent. Parliament set the threshold. Parliament decided that a certain level of criminal conduct should result in removal from Canada. A pattern of sentence manipulation to avoid that threshold does not reflect judicial independence — it reflects judicial override of a legislative policy decision.

The Liberal government has done nothing to address this pattern. It has not brought forward legislation to close the loophole. It has not directed the Crown to oppose below-threshold sentencing arguments in cases where the evidence supports a longer sentence. It has not publicly acknowledged the pattern exists.

The result is a justice system that says one thing and does another — and non-citizens who have committed serious crimes remaining in Canadian communities because the sentencing system has learned to step around the law's intent.

## The Committee: Where Accountability Went to Die

The most damning aspect of this story is not any single policy failure. It is what happened when Parliament tried to do its job.

The Parliamentary Health Committee called Minister Diab to testify on the intersection of immigration and healthcare. It is a legitimate oversight function. The committee has every right — indeed, every obligation — to probe whether the immigration system is being managed with any consideration for healthcare system capacity.

Minister Diab's initial testimony was so inadequate that Conservative committee members took the unusual procedural step of compelling her to return. Ministers are not routinely recalled to committees. This happens when testimony is materially incomplete, misleading, or both.

At her testimony, when asked directly how many more patients Canada's healthcare system could handle — a question that goes to the heart of whether immigration planning is being done responsibly — the Minister laughed and declared it "not a fair question for immigration."

This response deserves careful analysis, because it reveals a governing philosophy.

If healthcare capacity is not a fair question for immigration, then the immigration ministry operates in a complete silo — making decisions about intake levels and program benefits with zero accountability for downstream healthcare system impacts. In a country where healthcare is publicly funded, provincially delivered, and already at the breaking point, this is not a defensible position. It is a confession of policy recklessness dressed up as a jurisdictional objection.

The families of patients who have waited 14 hours in emergency departments that then closed for the night because there was no staff. The seniors driving three hours to find a doctor who will see them. The people rationing medications because they cannot afford the prescriptions their provincial plan won't cover. They did not get to laugh at the question of healthcare capacity.

## What the Numbers Tell Us

The scale of the problem requires quantification.

Canada received over 140,000 asylum claims in 2023 — a record. Historically, approximately 40 to 50 percent of claims are rejected at the IRB level. That suggests tens of thousands of rejected claimants in any given year — people who have been through due process and been found not to qualify for protection.

Canada removes far fewer people per year than receive removal orders. The enforcement gap between orders issued and orders executed is one of the most poorly reported and least understood failures in the immigration system. Rejected claimants can remain in Canada for months or years while removal proceedings grind through a backlogged system.

During that time, IFHP coverage continues.

The Parliamentary Budget Officer has flagged IFHP as a growing federal expenditure. The program has scaled up significantly since the Trudeau government restored and expanded it from the restricted Harper-era version. The specific cost of providing IFHP coverage to rejected claimants — the population that arguably should not be here — has not been publicly isolated by the government. That is a transparency failure that Parliament should remedy by requiring a detailed IFHP cost breakdown by beneficiary category.

On the other side of the ledger: 6.5 million Canadians reported not having a regular family doctor as of 2024, according to Statistics Canada's Canadian Community Health Survey. Dozens of emergency departments are operating on reduced hours or have closed temporarily across rural Canada. Healthcare worker burnout, driven partly by patient volume, is contributing to a supply crisis that will take years to address even under the best policy conditions.

In this environment, a federal program providing premium supplemental healthcare to rejected asylum claimants is not merely a policy question. It is a political and moral provocation — and the government's response has been to misrepresent the program at committee and laugh off questions about capacity.

## The Accountability Vacuum

What makes this story structurally important — beyond the specific details — is what it reveals about how this government manages accountability.

A minister makes a verifiably false statement at a Parliamentary committee. The committee is forced to recall her. She doubles down on evasion. The Parliamentary Budget Officer and her own department's documentation contradict her. And the Prime Minister's office says nothing.

This is not how a government accountable to Parliament is supposed to operate. Parliamentary committees are the primary mechanism through which the executive is held accountable between elections. When a minister misrepresents program details to a committee, Parliament's ability to perform its oversight function is directly undermined.

Minister Diab owes the committee, and Canadians, a clear and complete account of:

1. The exact eligibility rules for IFHP coverage for rejected asylum claimants, with specific citation of the governing policy documents
2. The annual cost of IFHP coverage for rejected claimants specifically
3. What modelling, if any, the immigration ministry has done on healthcare system capacity in relation to immigration levels
4. What steps the government is taking to accelerate removal of rejected claimants who have exhausted all appeal processes
5. What the government's position is on the judicial sentencing pattern described by Rempel Garner

Until those answers are provided clearly, on the record, the minister's testimony stands as a case study in the failure of ministerial accountability.

---

## What Needs to Happen

The solutions here are not complicated, even if the politics are. They require political will the current government has not demonstrated.

First, the IFHP must be restructured to remove premium supplemental benefits for rejected claimants. Basic emergency healthcare is a humanitarian minimum — no credible voice argues Canada should let someone die untreated. But dental, vision, and prescription drug coverage beyond basic medical need should not be provided to people who have been ordered removed. This is not cruelty. It is the correct prioritization of public resources.

Second, Canada must dramatically accelerate removal of rejected claimants. The enforcement gap is not a force of nature. It is a policy choice and a resource allocation choice. A government that takes immigration integrity seriously funds and staffs the removal system.

Third, Parliament should legislate to address the judicial sentencing loophole on deportation thresholds. If the six-month threshold is being systematically avoided through calibrated sentences, Parliament should either change the threshold, clarify the intent, or direct Crown counsel to address below-threshold arguments in serious criminal cases.

Fourth, the immigration ministry must be required to publish healthcare capacity modelling as part of its immigration level planning. The idea that immigration intake targets can be set without reference to healthcare, housing, or infrastructure capacity is a governance failure. Integration across ministries is not optional — it is the basic requirement of responsible government.

Fifth, Minister Diab should correct her committee testimony on the record. If she made an error, she should say so. If she made a deliberate misstatement, that is a matter for Parliament to consider under its privilege rules.

## Conclusion: The Laugh Heard Across the Waiting Room

Somewhere in Canada tonight, a family is in an emergency room waiting to be seen. Somewhere, a senior is three months into a wait for a specialist referral. Somewhere, a person is rationing insulin because the cost is too high.

And the federal minister responsible for a program that provides dental and vision coverage to rejected asylum claimants — people the legal system has said don't qualify to stay in Canada — laughed when asked how many patients the healthcare system could handle.

That laugh is the story. Not because it's mean-spirited, but because it reveals the governing assumption: that healthcare capacity is not immigration's problem. That the system will absorb whatever comes. That asking about limits is unfair.

It is not unfair. It is the most important question in Canadian domestic policy right now. And the Canadians waiting in those rooms — and those who can't find a room to wait in at all — deserve a government that takes it seriously.
